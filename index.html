From bench to bedside

From blood sample to patient: how antibody medicine is made and shared during a pandemic

Luca, Thom, Charlotte & Plinke
🦠 SARS-CoV-2🩸 Malaria🏭 Manufacturing🕰️ History

When a new disease appears, the world cannot wait years for a treatment. COVID-19 showed that a monoclonal antibody (mAb), a lab-made copy of a natural antibody, can go from a blood sample to an emergency medicine in under a year [1,2], but only if finding it, making it, and sending it out happen as one plan, not three separate jobs.

🧬

The biology behind the technology

What an antibody is and how it works

An antibody is a Y-shaped protein made of two heavy and two light chains. The tips of the Y are highly variable and fit one specific structure on a pathogen, called the epitope. The stem, the Fc region, tells the immune system what to do next. An antibody can neutralise a virus by blocking the part it uses to enter a cell, and it can mark the pathogen so that immune cells and the complement system destroy it [3]. Some therapeutic antibodies, such as AZD7442, have an engineered Fc region that makes them last much longer in the body [4].

Where antibodies come from

The body has a limited number of genes but must answer millions of possible targets. Each developing B cell therefore joins gene segments at random (V(D)J recombination), so every B cell carries one unique antibody [5]. During an infection, only the cells whose antibody fits the pathogen multiply, an idea known as clonal selection.

In the lymph nodes these cells then mutate their antibody genes, and the best binders survive (affinity maturation).

They become plasma cells, which release antibodies, and memory B cells, which persist for years. These memory cells are what scientists collect in step 1.

The pathogen's side

For SARS-CoV-2 the main target is the spike protein. Its receptor-binding domain attaches to the ACE2 receptor on human cells, so an antibody covering it neutralises the virus [7]. But viruses mutate, and a change in the target can make an antibody useless. This was the case with a COVID medicine which contained two types of antibodies (AZD7442), one half of AZD7442, was ineffective against every Omicron sublineage of COVID. Using a single antibody risks selecting escape variants [4]. That is why two antibodies that bind different sites are often combined. Parasites can be targeted too: in malaria, an antibody against the sporozoite stage, which invades the liver, reduced infections in children in Mali [8].

How antibodies were discovered

  • 1890: Behring and Kitasato show that serum from immunised animals protects against diphtheria and tetanus toxin, the start of passive immunisation [9].
  • 1950s and 1960s: Porter and Edelman work out the Y-shaped structure of the antibody [3].
  • 1975: Köhler and Milstein create hybridomas, cell lines that make one identical antibody. These were mouse antibodies, which people often react to. The first therapeutic mAb was approved in 1986 [3].
  • 1976: Tonegawa shows how V(D)J recombination creates antibody diversity [5].
  • From 1985: Phage display and humanisation remove the mouse parts [3]. Today, antibodies can be taken straight from human B cells.

Passive versus active immunity

A vaccine trains the body to make its own antibodies, which takes weeks but can last for years (active immunity). A mAb gives ready-made protection at once, but only for a limited time (passive immunity) [9]. This makes mAbs useful for people who respond poorly to vaccines or have just been exposed.

The six steps from blood sample to patient

1

Finding the antibody in a person's blood

The process usually starts with someone who has beaten the infection or been vaccinated. Their blood holds memory B cells. Scientists pick out the cells that stick to the right target, such as part of a virus's outer coat, using a glowing marker in a cell sorter or chip-based tools that check thousands of cells at once [10]. Each cell already carries one matching pair of antibody chains, so this gives a fully human antibody right away [3].

2

Reading the genes and picking the best ones

The antibody genes of the selected cells are copied and read by sequencing machines, giving hundreds of candidates. Computer tools check which are stable and easy to produce, and lab tests check which fight the disease best [1]. Only a few move forward, and two are often combined so the germ cannot easily escape [4].

3

Turning the gene into a real protein

The chosen genes go into production cells, normally Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells, which add sugar chains to proteins in a way the human body accepts. Setting up a stable cell line normally takes close to a year, but newer methods create working batches in a few weeks, turning a 12 to 14 month wait into under five months [2].

4

Making large amounts

Production grows step by step in bioreactors, from small batches up to thousand-litre tanks. Building a new factory takes years, so in an outbreak it is faster to use existing factories, if agreements were set up before the crisis, not during it [2].

5

Faster approval from health authorities

Fast-track routes, such as emergency use authorisation in the US or conditional approval in Europe, let a medicine reach patients on solid but smaller data, with more proof following later. Antibody treatments such as REGN-COV2 and AZD7442 (Evusheld) were authorised within about two years of the pandemic's start instead of the usual ten [4], because discovery, production, and review happened together.

6

Getting the medicine to the people who need it

A finished antibody is useless if it cannot reach a clinic. These medicines usually need to stay cold, so they need a reliable cold chain to the patient. Deciding who gets the first doses is also a public-health choice: governments and groups like the WHO usually prioritise the highest-risk people while more is being made.

📦
🔗

Why the full chain matters

None of these six steps is new by itself. In a pandemic they happen at the same time: production starts before the antibody is fully chosen, scaling up begins before approval is finished, and delivery planning starts before the first batch leaves the factory. Because pathogens mutate [4], a prepared system also needs a steady supply of new antibodies from recovered and vaccinated people. Being ready for the next pandemic is less about inventing new science fast, and more about keeping the right factories, partnerships, and fast-track rules ready to use.

Strong enough, long enough: what makes an antibody efficient?

Luca, Thom, Charlotte & Plinke
🎯 Potency⏳ Pharmacokinetics🔁 FcRn🩸 Malaria

Why does efficiency matter?

Malaria is still one of the world's biggest infectious disease challenges, with the majority of malaria deaths occurring among children in Africa. Despite existing prevention and treatment strategies, malaria is still a huge public-health problem. An antibody that provides immediate protection for several months could therefore be an important addition to existing malaria prevention strategies [1].

Two factors are particularly important: how much antibody is needed to block infection and how long protective antibody concentrations can be maintained in the body.

But what makes an antibody effective enough to provide this kind of protection? Two factors are particularly important: how much antibody is needed to block infection and how long protective antibody concentrations can be maintained in the body. The first relates to neutralising potency, while the second depends mainly on pharmacokinetics, including the half-life of the antibody.

🎯
🎯

Blocking infection through potency

SARS-CoV-2 provides a simple example of how neutralising antibodies work. The virus uses the spike protein to bind to the ACE2 receptor on human cells. Antibodies that bind to the spike protein can interfere with this interaction and prevent viral entry [3].

The more potent the neutralising activity of an antibody, the lower the concentration required to inhibit infection. This is important for passive immunisation because the amount of antibody that needs to be given and maintained in the body partly depends on the potency.

The same principle applies to malaria, although the target is different. CIS43LS and L9LS target the circumsporozoite protein, also named as CSP, of Plasmodium falciparum on the sporozoite stage. By binding to CSP, these antibodies interfere with the parasite's ability to invade hepatocytes. L9LS targets a conserved region of PfCSP and showed high neutralising potency in experimental studies [1,2]. High potency therefore matters because it determines how much antibody is needed to achieve protection.

⏳
⏳

Staying protected through pharmacokinetics

Imagine an antibody that is extremely potent at blocking infection but disappears from the bloodstream after only a few days. The protective window would still be short. An antibody therefore also needs to be persistent enough to maintain protective concentrations over time. This is where pharmacokinetics becomes important. A highly potent antibody requires a lower concentration to block infection. If it also has a long half-life, the concentration can remain above the protective level for much longer. This means that potency determines how much antibody is needed for protection, while pharmacokinetics determines how long that protection can be maintained.

IgG antibodies are continuously taken up by cells through endocytosis. Inside the acidic environment of the endosome, IgG binds to the neonatal Fc receptor, FcRn. This interaction is strongly pH-dependent. FcRn binds IgG at acidic pH and protects it from intracellular degradation. The IgG-FcRn complex is then transported back to the cell surface, where the physiological pH promotes release of the antibody back into the bloodstream. Instead of being sent to the lysosome and degraded, FcRn-bound IgG is therefore recycled. This recycling contributes to the relatively long half-life of IgG antibodies [7,8].

Scientists can further extend antibody half-life by modifying the Fc region. One well-known approach is the LS mutation, consisting of the M428L and N434S substitutions. These mutations enhance FcRn-mediated recycling, allowing more antibody to escape intracellular degradation and remain in the circulation. The M428L and N434S modifications have been shown experimentally to substantially increase antibody serum half-life [9]. A longer half-life means that antibody concentrations can remain above the level required for protection for longer. This can reduce the frequency of administration and make passive immunisation more practical. For malaria, this is particularly relevant because protection is needed throughout periods of high transmission. An antibody that remains protective for several months could therefore provide protection with a single administration [1,2].

📊
📊

From antibody design to protection

The importance of combining high potency with a prolonged half-life is illustrated by the study of Kayentao et al (2024) [1]. In this phase 2 trial in Mali, children aged 6 to 10 years received a single subcutaneous dose of the monoclonal antibody L9LS and were followed for 24 weeks. Participants received either 150 mg L9LS, 300 mg L9LS or placebo.

L9LS provided substantial protection against malaria. Efficacy against Plasmodium falciparum infection was 66% with the 150-mg dose and 70% with the 300-mg dose. Efficacy against clinical malaria was 67% and 77%, respectively, during the 24-week follow-up period. The subcutaneous route is also relevant for large-scale prevention. Compared with intravenous treatment, it is simpler to administer and could therefore make antibody-based malaria prevention more practical in endemic settings.

Earlier work with CIS43LS demonstrated the same principle. In a phase 2 trial among adults in Mali, a single intravenous dose provided 75.0% and 88.2% efficacy against P. falciparum infection at doses of 10 mg/kg and 40 mg/kg, respectively, over a six-month malaria season [2]. Together, these studies illustrate that an antibody must not only be highly potent, it must also remain effective for a sufficiently long period of time.

🧬
🧬

When antibodies lose their target

A long half-life is only useful if the antibody can still recognise the target. If a pathogen changes the structure of that target, an antibody can become less effective even when substantial amounts remain in the bloodstream.

This became clear during the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron. Mutations in the spike protein reduced the neutralising activity of several monoclonal antibodies. Case et al. showed that the effect differed between antibodies, with S309 remaining effective against several Omicron variants but with reduced potency against some strains [4]. This illustrates an important limitation, a long half-life cannot compensate for loss of antigen recognition.

HIV presents an even bigger challenge because of genetic variability. Studies of the broadly neutralising antibody VRC01 showed that antibody treatment can suppress antibody-sensitive HIV-1 variants, but resistant variants can emerge when viral replication is not completely suppressed [6]. Combining broadly neutralising antibodies with different targets can provide more sustained viral suppression and may help limit resistance [5].

The challenge in antibody-based prevention is therefore to combine potent targets with smart antibody engineering, turning a single injection into protection that lasts long enough to matter.

Conclusion

An effective antibody must be potent enough to block infection and persistent enough to maintain protective concentrations. FcRn recycling and LS mutations can extend antibody half-life, while the L9LS trial demonstrates how this can translate into protection after a single injection. However, long half-life alone is not enough, durable protection also depends on continued recognition of the target.

The challenge in antibody-based prevention is therefore to combine potent targets with smart antibody engineering, turning a single injection into protection that lasts long enough to matter.

Case studies

What's available now?

Luca, Thom, Charlotte & Plinke
🦠 SARS-CoV-2🩸 Malaria🧫 HIV

Monoclonal antibodies against infectious diseases

Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have become extremely important in treating and preventing humans from infectious diseases. Monoclonal antibodies are specific proteins designed to recognize and bind to certain antigens and molecules of a pathogen. Depending on which pathogen is bound by the antibody, the mode of action is different. Antibodies can interfere in almost every stage of the life cycle of a pathogen.

The SARS-CoV2 pandemic has accelerated the development and clinical use of monoclonal antibodies. On the other hand there has been growing interest in doing research into broadly neutralizing antibodies, for example against HIV. On top of that, there are antibodies being developed that target malaria parasites. The potential of antibody treatment seems endless [1].

🦠 SARS-CoV-2
🦠

REGEN-COV

During the SARS-CoV2 pandemic the REGEN-COV, a combination of the monoclonal antibodies casirivimab (REGN10933) and imdevimab (REGN10987), has been developed. Both antibodies in REGEN-COV target the receptor-binding domain of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. The only differences between both of them is that they bind different epitopes [2]. The antibodies are able to interfere with the interaction between the virus and the host cell receptor ACE2 by binding to the spike protein. In this way viral entrance into cells can be prevented [3].

A randomized, double blinded, placebo-controlled trial tried to answer the question, whether subcutaneous administration of REGEN-COV could prevent SARS-CoV-2 infection in individuals who had recently been exposed to an infected household contact. The study showed that participants were not protected for the infection, but participants who became infected after getting the REGEN-COV injection had a shorter duration of symptomatic disease. On top of that, they had lower viral loads compared to participants that only received the placebo. These results show that monoclonal antibodies can provide both protection against infection, and a reduction in viral replication and in this way also in transmission [4].

These results show that monoclonal antibodies can provide both protection against infection, and a reduction in viral replication and in this way also in transmission.

An important limitation of this study, however, is the dependence of antibody efficacy on viral mutation. Monoclonal antibodies recognize specific molecular structures on a pathogen. Mutations in or around the antibody-binding site can therefore reduce the efficacy of the treatment. This theory is relevant for a virus as SARS-CoV-2, because new SARS-CoV2 variants develop once in a while with substantial changes in the spike protein. As a result of this the effectiveness on neutralizing, SARS-CoV-2 depends not only on intrinsic neutralizing activity of the antibody, but also on the antigenic characteristics of the virus itself [4]

🩸 Malaria
🩸

CIS43LS

Monoclonal antibodies are not limited to viral diseases. They are used as preventive agents against parasitic infections, including malaria, as well. An example is CIS43LS (human IgG1), a long-acting monoclonal antibody directed against plasmodium falciparum. CIS43LS targets an epitope on the plasmodium falciparum circumsporozoite protein (PfCSP). PfCSP is the major protein present on the surface of the sporozoites and is needed for invasion of the hepatocyte in the liver. By blocking the sporozoites, the infection is blocked and onward transmission of malaria is prevented too [5].

A randomized controlled trial has been done in Mali to investigate whether a single intravenous administration of CIS43LS could protect healthy adults from a plasmodium falciparum infection throughout a six-month malaria season. The study included individuals that received either a lower dose of CIS43LS, a higher dose, or a saline placebo [5].

The results demonstrated that one single administration of the monoclonal antibody could provides protection against malaria over a period of six months. Rather than targeting a virus, CIS43LS interferes with the malaria parasite and prevents successful infection. The study also illustrates the potential value of long-acting antibodies in regions where malaria transmission is seasonal. A prophylactic antibody that remains active for several months could potentially complement existing malaria-control strategies [5].

🧫 HIV
🧫

VRC01 and combination therapy

Another type of infectious disease is HIV. Opposite to the design of an antibody therapy for SARS-CoV, HIV antibody therapy development already takes a very long time. HIV has a high level of genetic and antigenic variability, enabling the virus to rapidly generate variants that are less susceptible to a specific antibody. In this way the virus becomes resistant to the antibody, and the antibody does not work in a proper way anymore.

One of the neutralizing antibodies against HIV is VRC01. This antibody recognizes the CD4-binding site on the HIV envelope protein gp120. By binding to gp120, VRC01 can neutralize the binding of CD4 to this site. Therefore, the VRC01 is able to interfere with the interaction between HIV and the CD4 receptor on host T-cells [6].

Studies have demonstrated that VRC01 can reduce HIV replication in patient that have circulating viruses that are susceptible to the antibody. The same studies also showed that individuals with chronic HIV-1 infection, suppletion of a single infusion with VRC01 to these participants resulted in reductions in plasma viral load.

However, the study also gave insight in a limitation of antibody monotherapy. When VRC01 was given to patients that did not completely suppress viral replication, viral variants with reduced susceptibility to VRC01 could emerge. As a result, viral replication restarted again [6].

On top of that the study included participants that were already receiving effective antiretroviral therapy. In these participants, VRC01 did not produce a measurable reduction in the latent HIV reservoir. It is important to keep this in mind because suppressing circulating virus is fundamentally different from eliminating cells that contain latent HIV proviruses.

As described earlier the monoclonal antibody therapy also had a downside, therefore researchers started to investigate whether combinations of broadly neutralizing antibodies is an option as well. One important example of this is the combination of 3BNC117 and 10-1074, which recognize different regions of the HIV envelope protein. The rationale behind combining antibodies is relatively easy, if two antibodies recognize different viral targets, the virus may have greater difficulty acquiring mutations that allow it to escape both antibodies simultaneously [6].

In a randomized, double blinded controlled trial study published in Nature, participants received repeated injections of the 3BNC117 and 10-1074 monoclonal antibodies. Individuals that had viruses which remained susceptible to both antibodies, the combination therapy was able to maintain suppression of plasma HIV-1 RNA following interruption of antiretroviral therapy. In some participants viral suppression was maintained for almost one year [7]. The study therefore provides evidence that combinations of broadly neutralizing antibodies can produce sustained antiviral effects. On the other hand it demonstrated that pre-existing resistance against the virus remains an important factor in the success of antibody therapy. If the virus tested in a patient already contains variants resistant to one or both antibodies, the effectiveness of the combination may be substantially reduced [7]

🔗 Common thread
🔗

A common theme

The examples of antibody therapy in SARS-CoV-2, malaria, and HIV explain that monoclonal antibodies can be applied to very different types of infectious pathogens. For SARS-CoV-2, antibodies such as casirivimab and imdevimab can directly neutralize viral particles by targeting the spike protein. For malaria, CIS43LS targets plasmodium falciparum and can provide prolonged protection against infection with the parasite following a single administration. In HIV broadly neutralizing antibodies such as VRC01 and combinations with 3BNC117 plus 10-1074, demonstrate that antibody-based therapy can suppress viral replication. One common theme across all three diseases is therefore the relationship between specificity and pathogen evolution over time. The high specificity of monoclonal antibodies is one of their greatest advantages and implantations, because it allows them to target a defined molecular structure. At the same time, this specificity creates a potential danger, while changes in the structure, targeted by the antibodies, can reduce antibody binding and can lead to resistance.

Looking ahead

Futuristic perspectives

Luca, Thom, Charlotte & Plinke
💉 mRNA🎯 Bispecific🤖 AI design🛡️ Broad protection

The next generation antibody therapy

Monoclonal antibodies have shown in the last years that they can play an important role in the treatment and prevention of infectious diseases. The development of antibodies against SARS-CoV-2, for example, showed that new antibody therapies can be developed relatively quickly in response to an emerging disease. At the same time, the COVID-19 pandemic also revealed several limitations of antibody therapy. Viruses can mutate, causing an antibody to bind less effectively to its target and therefore lose its ability to neutralize the virus. In addition, the production, storage, and distribution of monoclonal antibodies can be complex and expensive.

For this reason, future research is not only focused on developing more powerful antibodies, but also on improving how they are designed, produced, and delivered. One promising development is the use of mRNA, which could allow the patient's own cells to temporarily produce a therapeutic antibody. Another approach is the development of bispecific and multispecific antibodies, which can recognize multiple targets at the same time. This could make it more difficult for a pathogen to escape treatment through mutations. Artificial intelligence (AI) is also increasingly being explored as a tool for designing and optimizing new antibodies.

The next five years of antibody research will not only focus on whether an antibody is effective, but also on whether it can be developed quickly, produced affordably, and made accessible to people around the world.

In the longer term, researchers are aiming to develop antibodies that can protect against multiple variants or even different strains of the same pathogen. This could be particularly important for rapidly evolving viruses such as HIV, influenza, and coronaviruses. Therefore, the next five years of antibody research will not only focus on whether an antibody is effective, but also on whether it can be developed quickly, produced affordably, and made accessible to people around the world.

mRNA-coded antibodies: your body as production factory

With a classic monoclonal antibody (mAb), a patient receives the antibody protein itself. With mRNA-encoded antibodies, the approach is different. Instead, the patient receives mRNA containing the instructions needed to produce the desired antibody. This allows the patient's own cells to temporarily produce the antibody. This process happens in several steps. First, the desired mRNA is designed to contain the correct instructions for producing the antibody. The mRNA is then enclosed in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which protect the mRNA and help deliver it into cells. Once inside the cells, the mRNA is used as a set of instructions to produce the antibody. Eventually, the mRNA is broken down by the cells. The aim is therefore to achieve temporary antibody production. Finally, the antibody can target the pathogen in the same way as a conventional mAb. [1]

One major advantage of mRNA-encoded antibodies is that they can potentially be produced more quickly and flexibly. Conventional mAbs need to be manufactured on a large scale, purified, and then distributed to patients. The mRNA approach can bypass some of these production steps, which could make it easier to adapt the therapy and produce it on a large scale. This technology has already been investigated for diseases such as SARS-CoV-2, Zika, Ebola, and rabies, mainly in preclinical studies. [1]

However, many questions still need to be answered before this approach can be widely used in humans. For example, how efficiently can the mRNA be delivered to the right cells? How long will the cells produce sufficient amounts of antibodies? And what immune reactions could be triggered by the mRNA or the LNPs? These uncertainties are some of the reasons why mRNA-encoded antibody therapy has not yet become a widely used treatment in humans. [1]

Bispecific and multispecific antibodies

Pathogens have different epitopes that can be recognized by different antibodies. Classical monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) usually target one specific epitope, which can eventually be altered by mutations in the pathogen. This can lead to the formation of new variants against which the mAb is no longer effective. In this way, the pathogen can escape the antibody. SARS-CoV-2 is an example of a virus that has continuously developed new variants through mutations. [2]

Because of this, researchers have started investigating ways to target pathogens at multiple sites. This approach is similar to the body's natural immune response. When the immune system encounters a pathogen, it does not produce just one type of antibody, but a collection of different antibodies that recognize different epitopes. Scientists can imitate this principle by combining multiple antibodies or by developing bispecific and multispecific antibodies. These antibodies can recognize and target more than one site on a pathogen, potentially making it more difficult for the pathogen to escape. [2]

A study investigated the safety and antiviral effects of a combination of three antibodies in people with HIV. The researchers selected three antibodies that target different sites on the HIV virus. In laboratory experiments, this combination was able to neutralize around 99% of tested HIV variants. The researchers then tested the three antibodies in combination in 12 people with HIV. In ten of these participants, the virus remained suppressed during the treatment period. This study demonstrates the potential of combining different antibodies to achieve broader and more sustained antiviral activity. [3]

AI: designing antibodies with the help of computers

Another promising development in antibody therapy is the use of artificial intelligence (AI) to design new antibodies. Developing and improving antibodies can be a long and expensive process because researchers have to test many different antibody candidates. AI can help make this process faster by analysing large amounts of information about antibody structures and predicting which antibodies are more likely to bind strongly to a specific target. [4]

Newer generative AI models can even design completely new antibody candidates. One technique discussed in the article is called a diffusion model. These models can generate possible antibody structures and sequences that are designed to interact with a specific target. Researchers can then select promising candidates for further laboratory testing. This could be especially useful during a new outbreak, because AI could help researchers identify promising antibodies much faster and potentially create antibodies with better binding properties or broader protection against different variants. [4]

However, AI-designed antibodies still need to be tested experimentally. A computer prediction does not automatically mean that an antibody will work in the human body. Therefore, AI is currently mainly a tool to support and accelerate antibody development rather than replace laboratory research. In the future, combining AI with laboratory experiments could make the development of new antibody therapies faster and more efficient. [4]

Broad and universal protection

A major goal for future antibody therapies is to achieve broader protection against different variants of a pathogen. Instead of targeting one specific version of a virus, researchers are looking for antibodies that can recognize parts of the pathogen that are less likely to change. Another approach is to combine several antibodies that target different epitopes. This can make it more difficult for the pathogen to escape through a single mutation. Recombinant polyclonal antibodies are another promising approach, as they aim to combine the broad response of natural antibodies with the controlled production of therapeutic antibodies. [2]

This could be especially important for rapidly changing viruses. However, broad protection does not mean that resistance can be completely prevented. The HIV triple-antibody study showed that combining three broadly neutralizing antibodies could maintain viral suppression in many participants, but some participants still experienced viral rebound. This shows that future therapies will need to account for existing resistance as well as new mutations. [3]

Critical appraisal

Despite these promising developments, several challenges remain. Antibody therapies can be expensive because developing, producing and testing complex biological medicines requires many resources. New technologies such as AI and mRNA could make development and production faster, but they do not automatically make these treatments affordable or accessible. [2, 4]

Logistics are another challenge. Conventional mAbs often require large-scale manufacturing, purification and distribution. mRNA-encoded antibodies could simplify some of these steps, and the technology may reduce some cold-chain requirements, but delivery, stability and safety still need further investigation.

Finally, pathogen resistance remains an important problem. Even combinations of antibodies cannot guarantee that a pathogen will not escape. Therefore, future therapies will likely need to combine several strategies rather than rely on one type of antibody alone. [1]

Conclusion

Over the next five years, antibody therapy could become faster, more flexible and more adaptable. mRNA technology could allow the body to temporarily produce therapeutic antibodies, while AI could accelerate the design of new antibodies. Combining multiple antibodies may provide broader protection and make viral escape more difficult. However, costs, logistics, safety and resistance will remain important challenges. The future of antibody therapy will therefore depend not only on creating more powerful antibodies, but also on making these treatments practical and accessible.
